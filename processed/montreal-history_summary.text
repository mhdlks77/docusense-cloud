**Summary of "The Role of the Parish in Fostering Irish-Catholic Identity in Nineteenth-Century Montreal" by Rosalyn Trigger**

This thesis examines how Montreal's Irish Catholics sustained a cohesive ethnic community in the 1800s, emphasizing the crucial role played by the Roman Catholic parish. Initially, the community focused institution-building efforts around Saint Patrick's Church, constructed in the 1840s to serve Irish Catholics. Later, the large Notre-Dame parish was divided, resulting in new, more localized Irish national parishes from the 1870s onward. These new parishes increasingly centered communal activity around various parish societies.

The clergy discouraged Irish Catholics from joining national societies that refused church authority, instead promoting organizations that combined religious and ethnic identities. This approach successfully integrated faith and community, as reflected in the development of broad parish-based societies and participation in public events like the Saint Patrick’s Day procession, which visibly mapped Irish-Catholic identity onto Montreal’s urban landscape.

The study demonstrates that the territorial (geographical) and social evolution of these parishes were closely intertwined, with parish boundaries shaping community life and identity. Through institution building, spatial reorganization, and the support of parish societies under clerical control, Montreal's Irish Catholics forged a distinct and enduring Irish-Catholic identity in the city.
